## Opening checklist — adopt and localize
Confirm designated responders and deputies; walk entrance, aisle, queue and cart-corral routes; inspect mats, seams, guards and cart straps; verify supplies and barriers; check open defects and closures; verify safe accessible and escape routes; review weather, restocking and cleaning plans; record time, findings and owner. Reopening a previously closed area needs its release, not just an opening checklist tick.
## Handover checklist
Outgoing lead communicates open hazards, isolated zones, temporary routes, incomplete clean-up, repair visits, product changes and missed checks. Incoming lead acknowledges ownership and records the next required action in the handoff log. Never leave "contractor will handle it" without a named responsible role and confirmation.
## Scenario A: wet entrance with curled mat
Expected response: protect customers and workers; keep safe access and egress; notify the lead; address water and the unstable mat under the approved procedure; verify before reopening; log the response and the source investigation. Wrong inference: more padding automatically fixes the hazard. Debrief: who owns saturation and replacement checks?
## Scenario B: guard partly detached after cleaning
Expected response: isolate the contact area as needed, prevent access to loose parts, preserve product/batch evidence and notify facilities. Do not push it back or use an unapproved adhesive. Review cleaning compatibility and attachment; inspect matching installations. Restart only after an approved correction and verification.
## Scenario C: customer reports wheelchair difficulty
Expected response: offer assistance through the site's accessible service process, record the barrier without unnecessary personal data, and escalate the route for review. Do not dismiss the complaint because a lab report passed. Stop use of a system that creates an unresolved access barrier.
## Scenario D: zero injuries in month one
Expected response: report counts and exposure honestly, inspect data completeness and balancing measures, and continue the planned review. Do not display a claim that the floor prevents injuries. Ask whether reporting changed and whether exposure is enough for any inference: zero events in 100,000 entries still leaves an upper bound of about 3 per 100,000 (document 35).
## Scenario E: a child-seat strap is broken
Expected response: take the cart out of service, tag it, log it in the hazard register without any customer details, and tell the cart-fleet owner. Check the rest of the corral. Wrong inference: the floor program covers cart falls.
## Scenario F: someone types incident details into the workspace
Expected response: when the workspace flags a name, age, injury or claim number, edit the entry to a coded reference to the protected system. If sensitive details were already saved or exported, tell the privacy lead, remove them from the planning record and replace any exported copies.
## Knowledge check and answer key
Can a warning sign alone isolate a hazard? No; use the site control procedure. Does playground testing approve a retail aisle? No. Who sets test thresholds? The competent reviewer, before testing. Who can stop use? Any trained worker starts isolation; the site manager suspends; the sponsor decides permanent withdrawal. Where do medical and claims details go? The protected organizational system.
