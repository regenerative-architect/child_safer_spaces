Final master prompt

Develop Child-Safer Spaces, a performance-based, material-neutral program to reduce slips, trips, falls and fixture-impact injuries in businesses children frequent, with access and safety for all customers and workers. Use the U.S. and Canada as initial frameworks, leaving explicit site-specific jurisdiction gates. Treat Walmart only as an example; imply no partnership.

Complete the work sequentially: define scope and stakeholder hypotheses; critically assess evidence and its transferability to children in retail; map laws, authorities and candidate standards; compare interventions, including the controls that could create new hazards; specify procurement and testing of the actual installed system; design a bounded pilot whose conclusions match its power; build an economic model with uncertainty, adverse outcomes and lifecycle costs; assign governance, decision rights and maintenance; prepare adoption materials; and deliver an operational offline HTML bundle.

Separate verified facts from recommendations, illustrative figures and unresolved decisions. Cite primary sources with jurisdiction, scope, edition, retrieval date and limits, and never imply foam, playground testing, or an absence of incidents proves retail safety. Do not invent approvals, efficacy, insurance savings, partners, signatures or funding.

Document 02, Business

Program decision and executive brief

Recommendation

Program recommendation: launch a voluntary, evaluated Child-Safer Spaces program. Start with documented hazards and operational controls; consider purpose-designed protective products only where a specific impact hazard survives simpler remedies. Do not install household foam tiles, pool noodles or improvised padding in retail aisles.

Purpose and scope

Reduce avoidable slips, trips, falls and fixture collisions in entrances, aisles, queues and waiting spaces, while keeping access and safety for every customer and worker. Planning population: visitors under 18, reported in the age bands of document 16, with all-visitor and worker balancing measures. Confirm age bands and legal definitions locally.

Decisions requested of the sponsor

  • Appoint a site lead, facilities owner and an evaluation lead independent of product suppliers.
  • Authorize an assessment budget and a capped pilot budget with a separate removal reserve (amounts and currency set by the sponsor; none is assumed here).
  • Adopt the gate sequence and the decision-rights matrix (document 49), including every worker's authority to isolate a hazard.
  • Permit publication of aggregate positive, neutral and adverse findings after privacy review.

Intervention order

First remove the hazard: repair leaks, broken transitions and obstructing fixtures. Next redesign exposure: rounded or recessed fixtures, layout and secure entrance systems. Then strengthen inspection, cleaning and response. Only then evaluate protective products where the remaining mechanism and operating conditions justify it.

What success means

A site can demonstrate that its chosen controls are installed as specified, maintained, accessible and monitored. A pilot can establish operational feasibility long before it can establish injury effectiveness: document 35 shows why a single-site 90-day pilot cannot support an injury-reduction claim. Neither participation nor this workspace creates a safety certification.

Site survey

Map each hazard by zone, mechanism, exposure, existing control, suspected injury route, photographs without people, and immediate action. Survey fixture edges from the floor up to at least the tallest planned child height: for children aged two to five, WHO growth standards put the 97th-percentile height at about 92–93 cm at age two and about 118–119 cm at age five [S37], so a 0–120 cm band is a reasonable program default for that age group. Raise the band for older children. Observe restocking and cleaning as well as trading, and review small-wheel and cane-tip interactions.

Risk triage — program method

Use severity 1–5 and likelihood 1–5 only to sort work. Describe each rating and its uncertainty; multiplying them is not a legal safety determination. Potentially serious hazards, blocked escape or accessible routes, loose parts and unstable walking surfaces are isolated immediately regardless of score. Other work gets an owner and due date; overdue actions escalate to the site manager.

Evidence gaps to commission

Child-retail incidence by mechanism with store denominators; effectiveness of edge guards and fixture redesign; loaded-cart and mobility performance of cushioned systems; aged, wet and cleaned performance; guard durability; life-cycle costs; and comparative injury outcomes. Assign these questions (document 32) before claiming effectiveness.

Document 15, Research

Pilot evaluation protocol

Workflow: W13 in document 49. Needs a privacy and research-ethics determination before structured data collection.

Design

Stage A is operational feasibility, with a suggested 90-day observation window after commissioning. It cannot by itself establish reduced injury rates. Stage B is a larger comparative effectiveness study only if feasibility and statistical planning justify it. An independent analyst sets sample size and duration from actual baseline rates, target effect, site clustering, exposure, attrition and acceptable uncertainty.

Illustration (document 35 and the Evaluation planning view): at an assumed 2 injury events per 100,000 entries, detecting a 20% lower rate with 80% power at a two-sided 5% level needs about 355 events in the comparison group — roughly 17.7 million entries per arm before any adjustment for clustering. A single store's 90-day pilot is far below that.

Use matched comparison sites or randomized/staggered introduction where feasible. Provide standard safety corrections everywhere. Pre-register components, primary outcome, analysis, stop rules and exclusions before outcome review. Record weather, promotions, traffic changes, reporting changes and concurrent renovations. A multi-component pilot estimates the package effect; it cannot isolate cushioning without a component comparison.

Outcomes

Primary effectiveness outcome proposed: reported fall events with injury per 100,000 visitor entries, all visitors. Report child events separately; use child-visit denominators only if measured credibly, otherwise label child events per total visits as a proxy. Track fixture collisions separately from falls and cart-related events separately from floor events. Secondary measures: severity bands, all falls, near misses, mobility barriers, staff push effort, sanitation defects, response time, downtime, restraint use where carts are in scope, and total cost.

Operational acceptance targets — proposed program defaults

Zero unresolved critical defects; all required approvals recorded; staff demonstrate critical procedure steps; inspection completion at least 95%; exposure-data completeness at least 95%; no unresolved access or sanitation barrier. These percentages are program proposals to adopt or change locally before the pilot; they are not drawn from any standard. Review deviations individually; aggregate scores never override critical failures.

Analysis

Report counts, exposure, missingness, rates and uncertainty. A rate ratio is not causal without design assumptions. For baseline changes, compare rate changes across groups and account for clustering and trends. Training and attention usually raise reporting, so a rise in near misses can reflect better reporting rather than more hazards. Zero events are not proof of zero risk. Report adverse outcomes even if injury counts improve.

Ethical oversight

Obtain the institution's research or quality-improvement determination before any human research [S21][S22][S41]; in Canada, pilot studies fall within the TCPS 2 definition of research requiring review where the policy applies [S22]. Obtain appropriate adult consent and accessibility accommodations; research with children needs separate ethics, consent/assent and safeguarding review. No induced falls or child impact experiments. Publish protocol deviations and conflicts.

Business case and financial model

Decision approach

Use the cost of operating a verified control, and treat injury savings as uncertain. Obtain supplier quotes and actual claims analysis before investment approval. Separate insured losses from costs the business retains; do not count an insurer's payment as a business saving. Program rule: assumed premium reductions are excluded. Count an insurance change only after the insurer confirms it in writing, and then only once.

Input schedule

Currency [ ]; study/assessment [ ]; design/permits [ ]; materials [ ]; substrate preparation [ ]; installation [ ]; closure [ ]; training [ ]; independent testing [ ]; advisory compensation [ ]; contingency [ ]; removal reserve [ ]; incremental annual maintenance and cleaning labour [ ]; annual retesting [ ]; replacements by year [ ]; annual retained addressable loss [ ]; assumed loss reduction [ ]; documented other savings and their basis [ ]; discount rate [ ]; evaluation horizon [ ]; early-withdrawal year and removal cost beyond the reserve [ ].

Formulas

Initial cost = all one-time implementation and assessment costs including the reserve. Annual net benefit = retained addressable loss × assumed reduction + documented, non-overlapping other savings − incremental annual cost. NPV = −initial cost + Σ (net benefit − replacement − removal + salvage in year t) / (1 + discount rate)^t. Simple payback = initial cost ÷ positive annual net benefit; otherwise no payback. With zero initial cost, "recovery" is not applicable. This simplified model excludes tax, inflation and salvage unless entered.

Illustrative scenario only — not a quote or an efficacy claim

Illustration: initial cost 15,000 currency units; annual added cost 2,000; annual retained addressable loss 20,000; horizon 5 years; discount 5%; no replacements or other savings. At 0% loss reduction, annual net benefit is −2,000 and NPV about −23,659. At 10%, net is 0 and NPV −15,000. At 25%, net is 3,000 and NPV about −2,012. Simple payback at 25% is 5 years, while discounted recovery takes longer. The five-year discounted break-even reduction is about 27.3% under these assumptions; it is not a predicted effect.

Illustration — early withdrawal: with the 25% case, if the system is withdrawn at the end of year 2 at a removal cost of 3,000 beyond the reserve, NPV is about −12,143. A no-go decision still has a cost, which is why the reserve and removal cost are planned before purchase.

Uncertainty

Enter low and high values for initial cost, annual cost, retained loss, reduction and discount rate in the Cost scenarios view. It shows how far each assumption moves NPV and the result when every assumption is pessimistic or optimistic together. Include a worsening case (negative reduction) for new trip, access or cleaning problems. Ranges are your assumptions, not probabilities.

Sensitivities and approval

Vary reduction, service life, replacement, cleaning labour and disruption. Record who owns each cost and benefit. Approve pilot spending for evidence generation only if the sponsor accepts the possibility of no financial return.

Privacy, records and information governance

Workspace boundary

This offline HTML tool stores optional planning edits in browser storage and exports plain JSON. It has no accounts, encryption, shared audit trail or access controls. Do not enter child identities, injury narratives, health information, witness details, CCTV, claims records or confidential contracts. Use approved organizational systems for those records and store only non-sensitive references here.

Built-in pattern check

When you save a register row, task evidence, gate note or site profile, the workspace looks for patterns such as email addresses, phone numbers, identity-style numbers, ages, injury terms, claim numbers and footage references and asks you to confirm or edit. Document drafts get a lighter check. It is a reminder, not a privacy review: it cannot recognise every name or detail.

Data minimization

Collect only information needed for the stated safety or evaluation purpose. Where PIPEDA applies, organizations should collect only the personal information needed for a legitimate identified purpose [S13]; other national, state and provincial requirements need local review. Do not assume consent alone satisfies every obligation. Avoid new child tracking, facial recognition or unnecessary video collection. Canada's privacy regulator advises considering less privacy-invasive alternatives before video surveillance, limiting use to the stated purpose, posting notice, securing recordings and deleting them when no longer needed [S42].

Pre-analysis plan fields

Rates and comparisons

Rate = events ÷ matching exposure × 100,000. Distinguish falls, falls with injury, cart-related events, collisions and injured persons. Report intervention and comparison counts and exposure with uncertainty. Use appropriate Poisson, negative-binomial or other justified models with an exposure offset and site/time structure; check overdispersion and clustering. A before/after difference alone does not remove seasonal or reporting effects.

Rare-event illustration

Illustration: assume a baseline of 2 injury events per 100,000 entries. A pilot with 100,000 entries would expect only 2 events. Under a simple Poisson model with perfect ascertainment, zero observed events give a one-sided 95% upper bound of −ln(0.05) ≈ 3.0 events per 100,000 entries — not zero. One observed event gives an upper bound of about 4.7. Clustering, ascertainment and exposure error make real bounds wider.

How much exposure would an effectiveness claim need?

Illustration (planning arithmetic, not a design): with the same 2 per 100,000 baseline, detecting a 20% lower rate (rate ratio 0.8) with 80% power at a two-sided 5% level, using the normal approximation for a log rate ratio with equal exposure, needs about 355 events in the comparison group — roughly 17.7 million entries per arm, about 177 times a 100,000-entry pilot, before any design effect for clustering. The Evaluation planning view repeats this calculation with your own inputs.

Sample-size commissioning

Specify the minimum effect worth detecting, acceptable error rates, baseline-rate uncertainty, cluster size and variation, between-site correlation, exposure forecasts, dropout and loss development. Ask the statistician for scenarios rather than one falsely precise number. If the required exposure exceeds available resources, state that the study is a feasibility pilot and choose operational outcomes accordingly. Do not present a powered design without these inputs.

Missing and changing data

Keep unknown age and severity separate; do not assign missing reports to "no injury". Define exposure-outage handling before analysis. Check whether reporting rises after training; interpret near-miss increases alongside safety culture and conditions. Keep a versioned correction log and a reproducible aggregate dataset.

Decision interpretation

Report confidence intervals, potential biases and external validity, not only statistical significance. Lack of significance is not proof of equivalence or of no harm. A significant package effect does not isolate cushioning's contribution. Report all pre-specified balancing outcomes and explain deviations. Decide scale, redesign, extension or removal using document 25.

Year-by-year economics and scenario interpretation

Extended model

The workspace supports annual operating costs and retained-loss assumptions, annual escalation, a scheduled replacement, terminal salvage and an optional early withdrawal. It shows each year's cash flow, discounting and cumulative position, the effect of your low/high assumptions, and the all-pessimistic and all-optimistic combinations. All inputs are user assumptions. No actuarial loss development, tax calculation, probability distribution or validated efficacy estimate is embedded.

Inputs and conventions

Initial cost includes design, approvals, testing, materials, installation, closure, training and the removal reserve. Year-1 retained addressable loss means the business's expected costs for mechanisms this intervention could affect, net of costs borne by others. Reduction may be negative to model worsening losses. Other savings need a written, documented basis; assumed insurance-premium reductions and insurer payments are excluded. Escalation applies to loss, other savings and operating cost from year 2. Replacement is a one-time nominal amount at the chosen year-end; salvage is a year-end amount at the horizon. If a withdrawal year is set, benefits and costs stop after that year, later replacement and salvage are cancelled, and the removal cost beyond the reserve is charged in that year. Discount rate and cash flows must be consistently nominal or real.

Calculation

Annual gross benefit in year t = (year-1 retained loss × assumed reduction + year-1 documented other savings) × (1 + escalation)^(t−1), while the system is in place. Net cash flow subtracts escalated operating cost, scheduled replacement and any removal cost, and adds terminal salvage. Present value divides by (1 + discount)^t. NPV includes the negative initial cost at time zero. First cumulative recovery is reported only when there is an initial cost; a later replacement can reverse it, so read the whole table.

Scenarios

Run worsening, no-effect, modest-benefit and favourable-benefit cases without treating any as a forecast. Evaluate shorter life, more cleaning labour, closure cost, recurring replacement, early withdrawal and lower retained loss. A discounted break-even reduction tells you the effect needed under the assumptions; it does not say that effect is achievable or likely. Above 100%, the modelled loss reduction cannot pay for the program; at or below 0%, documented other savings already cover costs.

Cost of evidence and equity

Include independent review, advisory compensation, accessible materials and staff time. These are real costs even if no injury improvement is detected. A sponsor can choose to fund learning or accessibility outcomes without claiming financial payback; state that objective explicitly. Keep societal benefits separate from business cash flows rather than adding them to ROI.

Review and export

Finance validates quotations, currency, timing and allocation. Risk/actuarial staff validate retained-loss inputs and loss development. The evaluation lead owns the uncertainty in the reduction. Export the scenario and the year table with assumptions so another reviewer can reproduce it.

Two-page brief for officials

The problem

Falls are the second leading cause of unintentional injury death worldwide, and nearly 40% of the disability-adjusted life years (healthy years lost to illness, disability or early death) lost to falls occur in children [S45]. Most child falls are not fatal: falls are a leading cause of non-fatal child injury [S46]. Shops, malls and other buildings open to the public are only part of that picture, and no international data say how large a part. Where retail data exist, ordinary hazards matter: in the United States, about 24,000 children under 15 a year were treated in emergency departments for shopping-cart injuries (1990–2011 average), most with head injuries [S31].

What Child-Safer Spaces offers

A free, offline planning kit for businesses and public buildings that children visit. It helps a site find and fix walking-surface, cart and fixture-edge hazards, check proposed changes against local rules, run a small accountable pilot, and decide honestly whether to continue. It is performance-based (it says what a fix must achieve and how it will be checked) and material-neutral (it recommends no product). It works without accounts, networks or tracking; planning data stay on the user's own device.

What it is not

  • Not a standard, a certification, legal advice or an approval. It never replaces building, fire, accessibility, workplace, consumer-safety or privacy rules.
  • Not proven to reduce injuries. No outcome evaluation has been completed. A randomized trial of soft flooring for older adults did not show fewer serious fall injuries [S8], and soft floors can make carts and wheelchairs harder to push [S35]. Playground surfacing tests do not transfer to shop aisles [S7].
  • Not a reason to collect data about children, carers or workers. The kit uses aggregate, non-identifying information only.

What adoption could look like

  1. You or a colleague read this brief and the adaptation kit (document 53). No commitment is needed.
  2. A local lead records which laws and standards apply and who must be consulted: disability organizations, worker representatives, families and businesses, including small ones (gate G2).
  3. A few sites run a voluntary pilot with named owners, a removal reserve and independent evaluation (gates G0 to G5).
  4. The sponsors decide in writing to stop, change or expand, using the results and their uncertainty (gate G6). Small pilots usually cannot show fewer injuries; document 35 explains why.

What you can do now

  • Share this brief with the people responsible for building, workplace, consumer-safety and public-health policy.
  • Name a contact who can answer "which rules apply here?" for pilot sites.
  • Offer a forum where disability, worker and family organizations can review proposals.
  • If you fund or convene pilots, ask for honest reporting of costs, adverse effects and null results, not for positive results.

Decisions that stay with you

Which laws and editions apply; whether any requirement should ever follow, and only through your normal consultation and rule-making; privacy and research-ethics rules; funding; and which language versions are official. Checkboxes in the workspace are never approvals.

Safeguards

No personal data, no surveillance, no enforcement against individuals and no blaming of parents or carers. A change that helps children must not make a space harder to use for wheelchair users, people with low vision, older adults or workers.

Country and organization adaptation kit

Who this is for

Officials, agencies, associations and organizations that want to use Child-Safer Spaces outside the United States and Canada, or in a large organization with its own rules. The rest of the package uses U.S. and Canadian laws and standards as worked examples. This kit shows how to replace them with your own without losing the program's safeguards.

What stays the same everywhere

  • Program rule: performance-based and material-neutral. Write what a fix must achieve and how it will be checked; never name a product as the answer.
  • Program rule: no safety change may reduce access for disabled visitors or make work harder or riskier for workers.
  • Program rule: non-sensitive planning only. No children's identities, injury or health details, footage, witness details or claim numbers in this workspace.
  • Program rule: decisions run through gates G0 to G6, and a checkbox is never a signature.
  • Program rule: honest evaluation. Report costs, adverse effects and null results; never claim fewer injuries without an evaluation that can show it.

Laws and their editions, standards and test methods, the authorities who decide, injury data sources, languages and sign conventions, consultation bodies, funding routes and procurement rules.
